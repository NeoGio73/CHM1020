Double Replacement Reactions

1Mix and observe

New to this? Read the prelab review first: how ions trade partners, what a precipitate is, and how to write a formula from the charges.

When two solutions of ionic compounds are mixed, their ions can trade partners. If one of the new pairs does not dissolve in water, it comes out as a solid: a precipitate. You will mix 36 pairs of solutions, one drop of each, on a laminated sheet and record what happens in each box.

Setting up the fume hood

Tip: turn your phone sideways for a bigger bench.

Rinse every drop off the sheet and start again? Everything recorded in Table 1 will be cleared.
  1. Using a pipet. Click a pipet, or its name in front of the rack, to pick it up. Then click a box on the sheet: one drop falls there. The pipet stays in your hand for the next box. Click anywhere off the sheet to put it back.
  2. With the AgNO₃ pipet, put one drop on each X in the top row.
  3. Add one drop of the solution named at the top of each column onto the AgNO₃ drop in that column.
  4. Pick up the toothpick and click each box to stir its two drops together.
  5. Click a box (with nothing in your hand) for a close look, and record what you see in Table 1: the color of the precipitate, or N.R. if there is no reaction.
  6. Do the same for the Pb(NO₃)₂ row, then CaCl₂, CuSO₄ and FeCl₃. Leave out the four boxes marked Skip.

What counts as a precipitate? A solid can look cloudy, milky, grainy or like a jelly, not only like an obvious powder. Two colored solutions that simply blend, such as blue and yellow making green, have not reacted if the drop stays clear.

In a real lab you would wear goggles and gloves. Silver nitrate stains skin dark brown. You would use a clean toothpick for each box, so that nothing from one mixture is carried into the next (cross-contamination). At the end you would rinse and wash the sheet, and wash your hands.

3Balanced equations

Write a balanced equation for each pair of solutions. The reactants and their ions are given. First decide which positive ion ends up with which negative ion. Then use the charges to write each product's formula, in the smallest whole numbers. Last, put in coefficients so that each ion is counted the same on both sides.

Type formulas with plain numbers, such as Ca3(PO4)2; the page shows them with the numbers lowered. Leave a coefficient box empty for 1. Count an ion made of several atoms, such as SO₄²⁻ or OH⁻, as one unit.
