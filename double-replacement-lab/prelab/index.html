1What is really in the bottle

A patient about to have a stomach X-ray drinks a chalky white liquid. Soap leaves a film on your hands in some towns and not in others. A kidney stone grows from a liquid that looked perfectly clear. All three come down to the same event: ions in water meeting a partner they would rather hold on to.

Every solution in this lab is an ionic compound dissolved in water. An ionic compound is made of two kinds of charged particles: positive ions, called cations, and negative ions, called anions. When it dissolves, the two kinds come apart and spread out among the water molecules. A solution of table salt, NaCl, is really Na⁺ ions and Cl⁻ ions moving about on their own. That is why it looks like plain water.

Cation

A positive ion, such as Na⁺ Mg²⁺ or Al³⁺. It is written first in a formula.

Anion

A negative ion, such as Cl⁻ or Br⁻. It is written second.

Polyatomic ion

A small group of atoms that stays together and carries one charge, such as SO₄²⁻ or OH⁻. Treat the whole group as one unit.

2Trading partners

Mix two of these solutions and four kinds of ions are suddenly in the same water. Each positive ion can now meet the other solution's negative ion. On paper, the ions trade partners:

AB + CD → AD + CBa double replacement reaction: each positive ion ends up with the other one's negative ion

What you actually see depends on the two new pairs.

One new pair holds together

If one new pair clings together more strongly than water can pull it apart, it comes out of the solution as a solid. That solid is a precipitate. The clear mixture turns cloudy, milky, grainy or jelly-like, and the solid may settle.

Both new pairs stay dissolved

Then all four ions go on moving about separately, exactly as before. Nothing has changed, so there is no reaction. You record that as N.R.

Acids and bases trade partners in the same way. When an acid meets a base, one of the new pairs is water.

    Then balance the equation

    With the right formulas written down, put a number (a coefficient) in front of each one so that every ion is counted the same on both sides. Count a polyatomic ion as a unit.

    Take MgCl₂ + NaOH → Mg(OH)₂ + NaCl. One Mg(OH)₂ holds two OH⁻, so you need 2 NaOH. Those bring two Na⁺, which pair with the two Cl⁻ as 2 NaCl. Balanced: MgCl₂ + 2 NaOH → Mg(OH)₂ + 2 NaCl.
