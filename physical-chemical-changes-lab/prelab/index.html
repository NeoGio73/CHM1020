Prelab review · Physical and Chemical Changes

Same stuff, or something new?

About ten minutes. Read it before the lab, practice describing three everyday things, then check yourself with the questions at the end.

1Two kinds of change

Tear a sheet of paper in half and you have two smaller sheets of paper. Burn it and you have ash and smoke. Both times the paper changed, but only the second time did it stop being paper. That is the difference this lab is about.

Physical change

The substance is still the same substance. Only its form has changed: its shape, its size, its state (solid, liquid or gas), or what it is mixed with.

Tearing paper. Water boiling into steam. Bending a wire. Stirring sand into water.

Chemical change

At least one new substance forms. Some of what you started with is used up, and something that was not there before appears. Another name for it is a chemical reaction.

Paper burning. Iron rusting. A match being struck. Bread dough rising.

A physical change can usually be undone by another physical change: cool the steam back into water, pick the sand back out. To undo a chemical change you would need another chemical change.

2Four signs of a chemical change

You cannot see a new substance being made, atom by atom. What you can see is evidence. When two things are put together and a chemical change happens, there is often (not always) one or more of these four signs.

◐

A change of color

Bleach taking the color out of a stain. A test strip changing color in pool water.

▣

A solid forms

When a solid appears out of two clear solutions it is called a precipitate.

Soap scum in hard water. Milk curdling when lemon juice is added.

◌

A gas forms

You see it as bubbles rising through a liquid.

An antacid tablet fizzing in water. Baking soda dropped into vinegar.

△

A change in temperature

The mixture gets warmer or colder by itself, with nothing heating or cooling it.

A hand warmer heating up. An instant cold pack going cold.

Signs are clues, not proof

Each of the four signs can also turn up in a change that is only physical. So a sign tells you to look harder and ask the real question: is there a new substance?

What you seeWhen it is only a physical changeWhen it is a chemical change
BubblesWater boiling: the bubbles are water, as a gas. Same substance.An antacid tablet in water: the bubbles are carbon dioxide, which was not there before. New substance.
A new colorBlue paint stirred into yellow paint looks green, but both paints are still there. Same substances.A copper roof slowly turning green: the green is a new compound of copper. New substance.
A solid appearsMelted candle wax going hard as it cools. Same substance.Two clear solutions mixed, and a cloudy solid comes out. New substance.

3When things only mix

If two things are put together and no chemical change happens, what you have is a mixture: two or more substances side by side, each still itself. Mixtures come in two kinds, and you can usually tell them apart just by looking.

Homogeneous

The same all the way through. Any spoonful you take out looks like, and is made of the same as, any other. You cannot see separate parts.

Air. Brass. Apple juice. Sweetened tea.

Heterogeneous

Not the same all the way through. There are separate parts, and one spoonful can be different from the next.

A salad. Sand in water. Granite. Orange juice with pulp.

Making a mixture is a physical change, and so is taking one apart: filtering sand out of water, or letting the water evaporate from salty water, gives back the same substances that went in.

4Say exactly what you see

Most of this lab is careful looking. For everything you describe, before mixing and after, give at least these three things, and then anything else you notice (its texture, its shape, whether it is the same all the way through).

State. Solid, liquid or gas.
Color. If it has no color of its own, say colorless.
Clear or cloudy. Clear means you can see through it. Cloudy means you cannot.

Clear is not a color. Apple juice is clear and it is also yellow-brown. Water is clear and colorless. Milk is neither: it is white and cloudy. So “clear” and “colorless” are two different observations, and a good description gives both.

Try it on these three. None of them is in the lab.

5What you will do in the lab

  1. There are seven numbered test tubes in a rack, and a row of bottles and jars behind them. Each tube gets one pair of substances.
  2. Before you mix a pair, you look closely at each of the two and record its state, its color and how it looks.
  3. You add each substance with the tool that stands in its bottle or jar: a dropper, a spatula, or forceps.
  4. For four of the tubes you also read a thermometer, before mixing and after.
  5. Three of the tubes are stirred with a glass rod.
  6. After mixing you look closely again and record everything you see.
  7. Then you decide, from your own observations, what evidence of a chemical change there was in each tube, and which kind of mixture you made in the others.
  • Why record “before” at all?You can only say that something changed if you know what it looked like to begin with.
  • Why a thermometer?Two colorless liquids can be mixed and still look like one colorless liquid. A change in temperature may be the only evidence there is.
  • Why keep looking at a tube?Some changes are over in a second. Others take several minutes to show. One of your tubes is slow: come back to it.
  • Why not shake or stir everything?Stirring is part of the method for some tubes and would spoil what you are watching in others. The lab page tells you which are which.

6Check yourself

Pick an answer for each. Nothing here is graded or saved.
