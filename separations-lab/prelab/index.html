Pulling black ink apart

About ten minutes. Read it before the lab, run the practice plates forward and back, work through one comparison, then check yourself with the questions at the end.

Open the lab →

1Black ink is not one thing

Write a line with a black pen and it looks like one color. It is not. The ink in most black pens is a mixture of several dyes, such as a blue, a red and a yellow, that together look black. Different makes of pen reach black with different dyes, so each ink has its own recipe.

That recipe is what you will use in this lab. A ransom note was written in black ink, and several black pens were found in the same room. If you can pull the ink from the note apart into its dyes, and do the same with the ink of each pen, you can see which pen has the same recipe as the note.

Mixture

Two or more substances together, each still itself. Ink, air and salt water are mixtures. The parts of a mixture can be separated without changing what they are.

Pure substance

One substance only, such as water or one single dye. It cannot be split into anything simpler by physical means.

Physical separation

Sorting the parts of a mixture without breaking any chemical bonds: filtering, evaporating, or, in this lab, chromatography. The dyes that come out are the same dyes that went in.

2How chromatography separates the dyes

A small dot of ink is put near the bottom of a plate, and the bottom edge of the plate is stood in a shallow pool of solvent. The solvent soaks upward through the plate, the way water creeps up a paper towel. When it reaches the dot, it starts to carry the ink with it.

Every dye in the ink is now pulled two ways at once:

  • The moving solvent pulls it along.A dye that dissolves well in the solvent spends most of its time moving, and travels far up the plate.
  • The plate holds it back.A dye that clings to the surface of the plate spends most of its time stuck, and stays low.

Each dye strikes its own balance between the two, so each one travels its own distance. The black dot pulls apart into bands or streaks of color, one for each dye. Letting the solvent climb the plate is called developing it, and the finished plate is a chromatogram.

How far a dye travels depends on the dye and on the solvent. Change the solvent, and the same dye may travel a different distance. That is why you will run every ink in two different solvents.

3Watch two plates develop

Here are two plates with the same five dots on each: four pens, A to D, and an unknown in the middle. One plate stands in acetone, the other in a mixture of isopropyl alcohol and water. Move the pointer (or your finger) across the picture to run the time forward and back.

These four inks are made up for practice. They are not the pens in your lab, and this unknown is nobody's unknown.

  • Nothing happens to a dot until the solvent reaches it. Then the colors start to move.
  • The pencil line does not move at all. Pencil does not dissolve in these solvents, which is why the plate is marked in pencil and never in ink.
  • The solvent climbs quickly at first and more slowly as it gets higher.
  • The run is stopped while the solvent is still a little below the top of the plate.

4Reading the plates

Run the practice plates to the end, then compare the unknown with each pen in turn. For each pen, ask the same question in both solvents: does this lane show the same colors as the unknown, at the same heights?

Decide for yourself which pen matches the unknown before you open the comparison below.

The unknown matches pen C. Notice that acetone alone would not have settled it: in acetone, pens A and C look the same. It took the second solvent to tell them apart.

The rule for a match

Two inks are the same only if they give the same colors at the same heights in both solvents. One clear difference in either solvent is enough to eliminate a pen. And it is possible for an unknown to match none of the pens it is compared with.

5What you will do in the lab

  1. The lab page shows the code of your unknown at the top. Each student has a different one, so the same pens are not on everyone's plates.
  2. Two plates are waiting, one for each solvent, with the ink from the ransom note already on the middle mark. You add a small dot from each of four pens, each above its own number.
  3. You make each solvent in a graduated cylinder: 6 mL of acetone, or of isopropyl alcohol, and then distilled water up to the 8 mL line. Each one goes into its own glass chamber.
  4. You stand each plate in its chamber and record the time on the timer.
  5. When the solvent is 1 to 2 cm from the top of a plate, you take the plate out and record the time again. A button lets you speed up the wait.
  6. You record the colors on each lane, compare the unknown with each pen, and decide which pen, if any, wrote the note.
  • Why pencil?Ink lines on the plate would separate and run along with the samples. Pencil stays where it is.
  • Why a small dot?A big blot spreads into wide, smeared streaks that overlap. A small dot gives narrow bands that are easy to compare.
  • Why must the solvent start below the dots?If the pool covered the dots, the ink would wash off into the pool, not climb the plate.
  • Why keep the lid on?Acetone and alcohol evaporate quickly. The lid keeps the air in the chamber full of solvent vapor, so the plate does not dry out as the solvent climbs.
  • Why take the plate out before the solvent reaches the top?Once the solvent has nowhere left to go, the dyes that travel fastest pile up at the top edge, and the pattern is harder to read.

6Check yourself

Pick an answer for each. Nothing here is graded or saved.
