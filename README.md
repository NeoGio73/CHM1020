# CHM1020

Course materials for CHM1020 that run in a web browser.

## Density of an Unknown Metal

A virtual lab set in a fume hood. Students weigh five pieces of an unknown metal on a digital
balance, add them one at a time to a graduated cylinder that already holds 26.2 mL of water, and
record the volume after each piece. The page then draws their graph of total mass against volume
of metal, and they work out the equation of the trendline from two points on it.

**Open the lab:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/density-lab/

**Prelab review:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/density-lab/prelab/ explains density, using
a digital balance and its ZERO button, finding a volume by water displacement, reading a meniscus
to 0.1 mL (with practice cylinders), and finding the slope, y-intercept and equation of a
trendline (with a worked example and a checked practice). It ends with ungraded self-check
questions. Everything it practises on is made up, so it gives away no unknown.

- Each student gets their own unknown, shown as a five-character code on the page and on the
  printed report.
- A gold outline shows which piece the pointer is on, and the balance or cylinder a carried piece
  would go to.
- Work is saved in the student's browser as they go, and the finished report prints on one page
  (or saves as a PDF).
- The page checks each reading and each worksheet answer and says so on the report.

**Sources**

- The densities of the metals an unknown can be were checked against the CRC Handbook of
  Chemistry and Physics, 86th ed. (Lide, D. R., Ed.; CRC Press, 2005), by Prof. Picione on
  2026-10-21. The lab's pages do not say which metals they are, so that none gives away an
  unknown.
- The in-person experiment is the metal-slug density lab of Daytona State College's CHM1025
  instructor key, which does not name the metals, so the choice of metals is the lab's own.
- The rest are the instructor's choices: the 26.2 mL of water already in the cylinder; the
  pieces' sizes (nominally 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.5 and 12.0 mL, each varied by up to ±0.6 mL from one
  student to the next); a mass counts as right within 0.011 g and a volume within 0.2 mL; the
  water climbs 0.9 mL up the glass above the true level, which is how a reading taken at the
  glass is recognized; and the tolerances used to check the graph and the equation.

## pH of Common Solutions

A virtual lab at the same fume hood. Students first predict whether each of twelve solutions
(distilled water, ocean water, shampoo, soda pop, vinegar, acid rain, lemon juice, window
cleaner, 0.1 M and 1.0 M NaOH, 0.1 M and 1 M HCl) is an acid, a base or neutral, and lock those
predictions. That opens the bench. They put a few drops of each solution in its own well of a
spot plate, dip a strip of pH paper in each well, and hold the strip next to a 0–14 color chart
to read its pH. They record whether each prediction was right, then rank the solutions from least
to most acidic.

**Open the lab:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/ph-lab/

**Prelab review:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/ph-lab/prelab/ explains what pH means and
why each step is ten times. It has an interactive pH scale with everyday examples (stomach acid,
canning, pools, hydrangeas, rain), shows how pH paper works, and ends with ungraded self-check
questions. Its examples avoid giving away the pH of the twelve lab solutions.

- Every student tests the same twelve solutions, with pH values taken from the results table of
  Daytona State College's CHM1025 instructor key.
- The bench stays closed until the predictions are locked, so predictions come before results.
- Work is saved in the student's browser as they go, and the finished report prints (or saves as
  a PDF).
- The page checks each recorded pH, each "prediction correct?" answer and the ranking, and says
  so on the report. The pH values
  themselves are compiled into the 3D lab rather than written in the page's script.

## Double Replacement Reactions

A virtual lab at the same fume hood. Students work on a laminated sheet marked with a grid: five
solutions down the side (AgNO3, Pb(NO3)2, CaCl2, CuSO4, FeCl3) and eight across the top (KI, NaCl,
NaOH, Na2SO4, Na3PO4, Na2CO3, CuSO4, FeCl3). They put one drop of each into a box, stir the pair
with a toothpick, look closely, and record what they see in each of the 36 boxes: the color of
the precipitate, or no reaction. From their finished table they pick out which ions tend to form
precipitates, and then they write balanced equations for eight of the pairs.

**Open the lab:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/double-replacement-lab/

**Prelab review:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/double-replacement-lab/prelab/ explains
what is in a solution of an ionic compound, how ions trade partners, and what a precipitate is.
It walks through six mixtures a step at a time, has a formula builder that shows the charges
cancelling, gives everyday examples (the barium drink before an X-ray, kidney stones, soap scum,
lead pipes, photographic film) and ends with ungraded self-check questions. It works none of the
pairs on the lab's sheet and gives no list of solubility rules, so the table, the questions and
the equations stay the students' own work.

- Every student mixes the same 36 pairs. A box is accepted only once both drops are in and
  stirred. Two boxes that are borderline in a real lab accept either answer.
- A gold outline shows which pipet, or the toothpick, the pointer is on, and the box a held one
  would be used on.
- The two "which ions" questions are checked against the student's own table. Each equation is
  checked for its formulas and its balancing, and the page says which part is off without giving
  the answer.
- Work is saved in the student's browser as they go, and the finished report prints on one page
  (or saves as a PDF).
- What each pair does is compiled into the 3D lab rather than written in the page's script.
- Based on the Double Replacement Reactions experiment of the Daytona State College Science
  Department.

**Sources**

- What each box shows, and the four boxes marked Skip, follow Table 9 ("Experimental
  Observations") of Daytona State College's CHM1025 instructor key, checked by Prof. Picione on
  2026-10-21.
- Two things are the lab's own choices, not the key's. The two borderline boxes (AgNO3 + CuSO4
  and CaCl2 + Na2SO4, which the key gives as no reaction) show a faint cloudiness and accept
  either answer. And in the question on positive ions, "almost every box" is taken to mean three
  quarters or more of the boxes in a row.

## Separations: Who Wrote the Ransom Note?

A virtual lab at the same fume hood, on separating a mixture by chromatography. A ransom note was
written in black ink, and several black pens were found in the same room. Each student has the
ink from the note, already on two plates, and four numbered pens. They put a dot from each pen
beside it, make two solvents in a graduated cylinder (6 mL of acetone, or of isopropyl alcohol,
made up to 8 mL with distilled water), stand each plate in its own glass chamber, and take it
out when the solvent is 1 to 2 cm from the top. As the solvent climbs, each black dot pulls
apart into bands of color. They record the times and the colors, compare the unknown with each
pen in both solvents, and decide which pen, if any, wrote the note.

**Open the lab:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/separations-lab/

**Prelab review:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/separations-lab/prelab/ explains why black
ink is a mixture, how chromatography pulls its dyes apart, and how to compare two plates. It has
an animation of two plates developing that can be run forward and back, a worked comparison, and
ungraded self-check questions. The inks in it are made up, so it gives away no one's result.

- Each student gets their own unknown, shown as a five-character code on the page and on the
  printed report. Different students have different pens on their plates, and for some the right
  answer is that none of the four pens wrote the note.
- The page checks the recorded times and, loosely, the colors recorded for each lane. It does not
  say whether the pen a student chose is the right one: that is for the instructor to mark.
- A picture of the student's own developed plates is printed with the report.
- A gold outline shows which pen, bottle, plate or chamber the pointer is on, and what a held one
  would be used on. The solvents are drawn as real liquid, which stays level when a bottle or the
  cylinder is tipped to pour.
- Work is saved in the student's browser as they go, and the finished report prints (or saves as
  a PDF). The wait while the plates develop can be sped up.
- Which pens and which unknown a code stands for, and what each ink separates into, are compiled
  into the 3D lab rather than written in the page's script.
- Based on the experiment "Separations: Who Wrote the Ransom Note?" of the Daytona State College
  Science Department.

**Sources**

- The two solvents (6 mL of acetone, or of isopropyl alcohol, made up to 8 mL with distilled
  water) and the rule for taking a plate out (when the solvent is 1 to 2 cm from the top) follow
  steps 7, 10 and 14 of the experiment's handout (Daytona State College, revised 8/1/2022).
- What each pen's ink separates into was read from real paper strips developed in this
  experiment: pens 1 to 4 from photographs, pens 5 to 8 from a video. The lab draws these
  paper-strip results on plates.
- The development times were measured in the in-person laboratory on 2026-10-14, one plate per
  solvent. The page says so.
- Four things are the instructor's choices: each student's plates carry any four of the eight
  pens; one unknown in five matches none of the four; a time in Table 1 counts as right within 20
  seconds of what the timer showed; and in the color check, a color must be ticked if one of its
  bands is strong (0.6 or more on the lab's scale of 0 to 1), while a ticked color counts as there
  if one of its bands has a strength of at least 0.2.

## Physical and Chemical Changes

A virtual lab at the same fume hood, on telling a physical change from a chemical one. Students
put seven pairs of substances together, one pair to a test tube: copper wire with silver nitrate,
sodium hydroxide with hydrochloric acid, zinc powder with hydrochloric acid, sodium carbonate with
calcium chloride, vinegar with water, vegetable oil with water, and sodium chloride with water.
They look closely at each substance before mixing and at each tube afterwards, read a thermometer
in four of the tubes, stir three of them, and record what they see. From their own observations
they then name the evidence of chemical change in each reaction, classify three mixtures as
homogeneous or heterogeneous, and classify nine everyday events.

**Open the lab:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/physical-chemical-changes-lab/

**Prelab review:** https://neogio73.github.io/CHM1020/physical-chemical-changes-lab/prelab/
explains the difference between a physical and a chemical change, the four signs of a chemical
change (and why each is a clue, not proof), the two kinds of mixture, and how to describe what
you see: state, color, clear or cloudy. It has three everyday things to practice describing and
ungraded self-check questions. None of its examples is a mixture from the lab.

- Every student mixes the same seven pairs. The page says what to do next and never what will be
  seen: seeing it is the lab.
- For each substance and each tube, students pick its state, its color and how it looks from
  short lists, which the page checks, and describe what they saw in their own words, which it
  does not: that is for the instructor to read.
- A thermometer takes the place of feeling a tube with the fingers. One tube changes slowly, and
  the wait can be sped up.
- A picture of the student's own seven tubes is printed with the report.
- Work is saved in the student's browser as they go, and the finished report prints (or saves as
  a PDF).
- What each substance looks like, what happens in each tube and every answer the page checks are
  compiled into the 3D lab rather than written in the page's script.
- Based on the Physical and Chemical Changes experiment of the Daytona State College Science
  Department.

**Sources**

- What is accepted for each substance before mixing and for each tube after it, the evidence of
  chemical change in each reaction, the three mixtures and the nine everyday events follow
  Daytona State College's CHM1025 instructor key: its Table 3 ("Evidence of Chemical Change"),
  its Table 2 ("Physical Changes & Classification of Mixtures") and its answers to the data
  analysis. Where the key accepts a second answer, so does the lab, and where the key says what
  to tell the student, the page tells them.
- How the tubes look on the bench was matched to the instructor's demonstration video of the
  in-person lab ("CHM1025L", https://youtu.be/c7LD9312NUc), except the sodium carbonate and
  calcium chloride tube, which follows the 2023 handout (the video used copper(II) sulfate).
- The amounts follow the experiment's handout (Daytona State College, revised 5/5/2023).
- The peak temperatures were measured in the in-person laboratory on 2026-09-30; the
  thermometer's time course is modelled. The page says so.
- Three things are the instructor's choices: a temperature counts as right within 1.0 °C of what
  the thermometer showed; the slow tube can be recorded after about 3½ minutes and has finished
  changing after about 15; and "Speed up the wait" runs the clock 40 times faster.
