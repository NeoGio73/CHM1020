1What density is

Hold a brick in one hand and a loaf of bread of the same size in the other. The brick is far heavier, because far more mass is packed into the same space. Density measures exactly that: how much mass there is in each unit of volume.

density = massvolume

Mass is measured in grams (g). Volume is measured in milliliters (mL) or cubic centimeters (cm³). So a density is a number of grams per milliliter, g/mL, or grams per cubic centimeter, g/cm³.

1 mL and 1 cm³ are the same amount of space

A cube 1 cm along each edge holds exactly 1 mL. So g/mL and g/cm³ are one unit with two names: a density of 1.20 g/mL is also 1.20 g/cm³. Liquids are usually given in g/mL and solids in g/cm³, but either is correct.

Some everyday densities

  • Airabout 0.0012 g/mLAbout a thousand times less dense than water.
  • Cooking oilabout 0.92 g/mLFloats on the vinegar in salad dressing.
  • Ice0.92 g/cm³Floats in a drink, mostly under the surface.
  • Water1.00 g/mLThe one to compare with.
  • Honeyabout 1.4 g/mLSinks to the bottom of a cup of tea.

Anything less dense than water floats on it, and anything denser sinks.

Density belongs to the material, not to the piece

Cut a block in half and each half has half the mass and half the volume, so mass divided by volume stays the same. A thimble of water and a bathtub of water both have a density of 1.00 g/mL. That is why density helps tell one material from another, and why, in the lab, five pieces of different sizes cut from the same metal should all point to the same density.

A made-up plastic block has a mass of 18.0 g and a volume of 15.0 cm³.
density = 18.0 g ÷ 15.0 cm³ = 1.20 g/cm³
That is more than water’s 1.00 g/cm³, so the block sinks in water.

2Mass on a digital balance

The lab’s balance is a digital balance with a flat pan on top. It shows mass in grams to two places after the decimal point, which is to the nearest 0.01 g, a hundredth of a gram.

  • Start from 0.00. With the pan empty, the display should read 0.00 g. If it does not, press ZERO.
  • One piece at a time. The pan can hold several pieces, and then the display shows them all together.
  • Let the number settle. Like a real balance, the display moves for a moment after something is put on or taken off. Read it once it stops changing.
  • Record every digit, and the unit. If the display shows 15.40, write 15.40 g, not 15.4 g. The last 0 is a measurement too: it says the hundredths were read, and were zero.

What ZERO does

ZERO (on some balances it is called TARE) makes the display read 0.00 with whatever is on the pan at that moment, and from then on leaves that out of every reading. Chemists use it to weigh a sample in a container: put the empty container on, press ZERO, then add the sample. In this lab there is no container, so press ZERO only with the pan empty.

3Volume by water displacement

A lump of metal has no scale printed on it, and its shape may be awkward to measure with a ruler. Water can do the measuring instead. When a piece sinks into water, it pushes aside (displaces) its own volume of water, and the water level rises by exactly that much. As long as the piece is completely under water, the rise in the level is the volume of the piece.

volume of the piece = reading after − reading before

Working out a volume this way, as the difference between two readings, is called finding it by difference.

The pieces pile up

In the lab the pieces stay in the cylinder. Each new piece goes in on top of the ones already there, and the level climbs again. The reading minus the starting 26.2 mL is then the volume of all the metal in the cylinder so far, and the mass that goes with it is the sum of the masses of all those pieces. Those two running totals are what you will graph.

4Reading the cylinder

Water clings to glass. Next to the wall it creeps up a little, so the surface of water in a narrow cylinder is not flat but curved, lowest in the middle. This curved surface is the meniscus. The volume is read at the bottom of the curve.

  1. Eye level. Look straight across at the bottom of the curve. From above, the reading comes out too high; from below, too low. In the lab, the Cylinder close-up view puts your eye level with the water for you.
  2. Know the marks. The lab’s 100 mL cylinder has a line for every 1 mL, a longer line every 5 mL, and a number every 10 mL. Find the numbered mark nearest the bottom of the curve and count the lines from it.
  3. Estimate one more digit. Between two 1 mL lines, judge how many tenths of the way up the bottom of the curve sits. In the picture it is about 4 tenths of the way from 42 to 43, so the reading is 42.4 mL.
  4. Always one digit after the point. If the bottom of the curve sits right on a line, the tenths are zero: write 61.0 mL, not 61 mL.

5Graphing mass against volume

Once all five pieces are in, you have five pairs of numbers: the volume of metal in the cylinder, and the total mass of that metal. A graph of one against the other shows at a glance whether they keep in step.

Which goes where

  • x axis (across): volume of metal in the cylinder, in mL. This is what you build up, piece by piece.
  • y axis (up): total mass of metal in the cylinder, in g. This is what comes with it.
  • Each point is one row of the table. For alloy K, the point after piece 3 is 7.0 mL across and 39.23 g up.

Why the points fall on a line

Every bit of one metal has the same density. Twice the volume of metal has twice the mass; three times the volume, three times the mass. When two quantities grow in proportion like this, a graph of one against the other is a straight line. With no metal in the cylinder there is no volume and no mass, so the line heads for the corner of the graph, the point (0, 0).

Why a trendline

Real readings carry small errors, such as a tenth of a milliliter here or there on the cylinder. So the points will not sit exactly on one line. A trendline, or line of best fit, is the one straight line that passes as close as it can to all the points together. It averages out the small errors, which is also why it may miss (0, 0) by a little, and may not pass exactly through any of the points. The lab page draws the trendline for you.

6Slope, y-intercept and the equation

Every straight line can be written as an equation of this form:

y = mx + b m is the slope; b is the y-intercept

The slope says how steeply the line climbs. The y-intercept is where the line crosses the y axis, at x = 0. In the lab you find both from the trendline, in four steps.

Step 1. Two points on the line

Pick two points that lie on the trendline, not two of your data points. The trendline stands for all five readings together, while any one data point carries its own small error. Choose spots where the line crosses a corner of the grid, so both numbers can be read cleanly, and take them far apart: the farther apart they are, the less a small misreading matters. Write each number with its unit, such as x1 = 1.0 mL and y1 = 6.0 g.

Step 2. The slope

m = y2 − y1x2 − x1 the rise divided by the run

Subtract in the same order on top and bottom. The units come along with the numbers: grams divided by milliliters gives g/mL. A slope in g/mL is a mass per volume, which is a density. That is the point of the graph: the slope of the trendline is the density of the metal, worked out from all five pieces at once.

Step 3. The y-intercept

Put the slope and one of your two points into y = mx + b and solve for b:

b = y1 − m × x1

The milliliters cancel (g/mL × mL = g), so b is a mass, in g. It should come out small, close to zero, and it may be negative.

Step 4. The equation

Write y = mx + b with your own numbers, each with its units: y = (slope with units)x + (y-intercept with unit).

Worked example: alloy K

  1. Two points on the line. P1 = (1.0 mL, 6.0 g) and P2 = (11.0 mL, 62.0 g). The line crosses a grid corner at both.
  2. Slope. m = (62.0 g − 6.0 g) ÷ (11.0 mL − 1.0 mL) = 56.0 g ÷ 10.0 mL = 5.60 g/mL
  3. y-intercept. b = 6.0 g − (5.60 g/mL × 1.0 mL) = 6.0 g − 5.6 g = 0.4 g
  4. Equation. y = (5.60 g/mL)x + 0.4 g

A quick check: put the other point’s x into the equation. 5.60 × 11.0 + 0.4 = 62.0, which is P2’s y. The equation fits both points.

7At the virtual bench

  • Your unknown. The five-character code at the top of the lab page decides which metal your pieces are made of. It is printed on your report.
  • The bench. A balance that reads to 0.01 g, a tray of five numbered pieces, and a 100 mL graduated cylinder that already holds 26.2 mL of water.
  1. Drag a piece with the mouse, or with a finger on a touch screen. Let go over the balance pan to weigh it, or over the cylinder to drop it in. Let go anywhere else and it goes back to the tray.
  2. Weigh first. A piece cannot go into the water until it has been on the balance. To zero the balance, click its ZERO button.
  3. Four views: Fume hood, Bench, Balance and Cylinder close-up, from the buttons under the 3D view. In the Bench view, a click on the cylinder or on the balance’s display also takes you in close.
  4. Read every volume in the Cylinder close-up. It puts your eye level with the water, and follows the water as it rises.
  5. Record as you go in the “Recorded data” table, then press Enter or click outside the box. The page checks each entry against the bench. A wrong one turns red, with a note saying what kind of slip it looks like, but never the right number.
  6. Reset bench takes every piece back to the tray, sets the water back to 26.2 mL and clears your recorded volumes (not the masses). It asks first.
  7. Your work is saved in this browser as you go, so come back in the same browser on the same computer. “Start over with a new unknown” erases everything and gives you a different unknown: use it only if you mean to.
  8. The graph appears once all ten readings are accepted. Click on it to drop a guide onto the trendline, with dashed lines to both axes, to help you read a point.
  9. The worksheet opens once the graph is drawn. Show your working in the large boxes: they are printed with your report but not checked.
  10. Turn in. Type your name and press “Print or save as PDF”. In the print window, choose a printer, or “Save as PDF” to make a file. The report shows your code, data, graph and worksheet, and how many entries the page flagged before you corrected them.
