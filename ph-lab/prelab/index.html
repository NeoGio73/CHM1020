1What pH tells you

Whether a pickle is safe to keep on a shelf, whether your pool water keeps you healthy, whether a hydrangea blooms blue or pink: all of these come down to pH, a number that says how acidic or basic a watery solution is.

Acids put extra hydrogen ions (H+) into water. Bases do the opposite: they pull hydrogen ions out, often by adding hydroxide ions (OH−) that grab them. pH counts how many hydrogen ions are around, but backwards: the more hydrogen ions, the lower the pH. Everyday solutions fall between about 0 and 14.

Acidic

pH below 7

  • Taste sour: think pickles, yogurt and sour candy.
  • Fizz when they meet baking soda.
  • The lower the number, the stronger the acid.

Neutral

pH exactly 7

  • Neither acidic nor basic: the hydrogen and hydroxide ions balance.
  • The middle of the scale.

Basic (also called alkaline)

pH above 7

  • Taste bitter and feel slippery, like soap.
  • Many cleaning products, like bleach, are bases.
  • The higher the number, the stronger the base.

Never taste or touch lab chemicals. People worked out "acids are sour, bases are bitter" centuries ago by tasting things. Strong acids and strong bases burn skin and eyes, which is why real labs use goggles and gloves.

2Every step is ten times

pH is not like a ruler where every step is the same size. Each step down the scale means ten times more hydrogen ions. So pH 5 is 10 times as acidic as pH 6, and 100 times as acidic as pH 7. A small change in the number is a big change in the solution.

    Why a "tiny" change can matter

    Because the scale works in tens, even a drop of 0.1 pH unit means about 100.1 ≈ 1.26 times as many hydrogen ions: roughly a quarter more. (Ocean scientists round it to 30%.)

    That is why ocean scientists worry about the sea's slowly falling pH. As the ocean soaks up carbon dioxide from the air, it forms carbonic acid. That makes it harder for corals, clams, oysters and some plankton to build their shells and skeletons. You'll measure ocean water in the lab.

    Sources: WHOI, EPA

        5How you'll measure pH

        An indicator is a dye that changes color with pH. You can make one in your kitchen: the purple juice from boiled red cabbage turns red or pink in acids, stays purple or blue in neutral solutions, and turns green or yellow in bases.

        • Red / pinkacid
        • Purple / blueneutral
        • Green / yellowbase

        pH paper works the same way, but it is soaked in a mix of several indicators, each changing color over a different part of the scale. Together they give a different color for each pH from 0 to 14.

        In the virtual lab

        1. Lock in your predictions first. A prediction doesn't have to be right; the experiment tests it.
        2. Put a few drops of each solution in its own well, using the pipette with the same number.
        3. Dip a fresh strip into the well, then wait about 5 seconds for the color to settle.
        4. Hold the wet end of the strip next to the color chart and find the closest color. Record that whole number.
